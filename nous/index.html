Why Nous

Open intelligence needs an inspectable trust boundary.

Nous's commitment to open, broadly accessible intelligence is why this work matters to me. Hermes already creates skills from experience, exports trajectories, and is testing evolutionary improvement. That makes Nous the rare place where Faber can be tested against real open-agent workflows—and where a negative result would still be useful.

Proposed work

Faber for Hermes: earn promotion before learning.

An agent may propose code, a skill, or a verification plan, but it should not be able to expand its own authority or choose the evidence sufficient to accept its work. I propose to test whether Faber can bind each candidate to maintainer-approved policy, a fixed environment, executed evidence, and a deterministic disposition—on top of Nous's existing tests, benchmarks, sandboxing, and human review.

This is an open application to pursue Faber with the Nous team, not an application to a listed opening. Faber is pre-existing public work. I am asking whether Nous would hire me to test, integrate, and—if the evidence supports it—build it with the team.

Days 1–30

Verify the verifier

Reproduce the Faber alpha and obtain fresh independent review of its four P0 remediation candidates. Map one bounded Hermes code or skill-promotion path, define a shadow integration, and freeze the strongest current Nous workflow as the comparator.

Days 31–60

Falsify the advantage

Compare both systems on held-out, existing-CI-passing candidates with the same model, context, sandbox, and budget. Measure false accepts, false blocks, referrals, reproducibility, maintainer setup and update time, and whether a thin integration matches Faber with less burden.

Days 61–90

Permission the learning loop

Only if the acceptance layer survives, attach receipt identities and failure classifications to exported trajectories. Test the authority and consent rules needed before those outcomes inform evaluation, self-improvement, or training. Finish with a continue, narrow, open-source, or stop decision.

01

Faber Proof

Evidence-bound acceptance for agent-written patches.

I designed and built, using Codex as an implementation agent, a local Python alpha in which model-generated proof plans are advisory. Repository-approved capabilities execute bounded checks; evidence is bound to the exact task, patch, policy, and environment; deterministic logic returns PASS, BLOCK, or HUMAN_REVIEW.

In a constructed scheduler case, ordinary tests accept both a flawed patch and its repair. Faber blocks the flaw with a concrete boundary-condition counterexample and accepts the repair.

Current local checkpoint: 725 passing tests, one guarded live-provider test skipped, and a 49-case deterministic adversarial regression campaign. The audit remains non-green: four P0 findings have remediation candidates and targeted regression tests, but await fresh independent re-verification. This is development evidence, not production testing or a claim of general program correctness.

02

Nous hackathon agent-bounty prototype

The lineage of Faber.

Faber began as an agent-native bounty market submitted to a Nous/NVIDIA/Stripe hackathon. It reserved tasks, evaluated exact commits against protected verifier targets, produced commit-bound acceptance receipts, and made payout and reconciliation idempotent. The larger idea was to turn completed, verified agent work into trustworthy learning trajectories.

A member of the Nous team responded especially strongly to the concept. I later narrowed it to Faber Proof because the marketplace and trajectory thesis depends on a harder prerequisite: can the outcome itself be trusted?

03

Hermes workflow case

Reconciliation before confidence.

Using Hermes, I built and validated a public-source ingestion workflow, then treated reconciliation as an evaluation problem rather than trusting the first successful run. It used twscrape for acquisition and Nitter as an independent cross-check, preserved upstream IDs and provenance, rejected contextual parents, committed per account, and advanced checkpoints only after successful transactions.

In the first 72-hour validation window, two result pages yielded 43 parsed source records and 30 canonical target-author activities. Nitter’s 24 IDs were an exact 24/30 subset after repost-wrapper canonicalization; an idempotence rerun added zero records. I also reversed my initial “zero results” diagnosis after finding that conversation modules were not strictly ordered.
